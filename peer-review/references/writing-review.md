# Writing the Review Report

A useful review helps both the editor decide and the authors improve the work.

## Use a summary → major → minor structure

### Summary

Briefly state:

- what you believe the paper claims,
- the main approach,
- the strongest aspect,
- the central concern if one exists.

This gives authors a test of whether the manuscript communicated what they intended.

### Major comments

Reserve major comments for issues that affect validity, interpretation, reproducibility, or the main contribution.

For each major comment include:

1. the specific issue,
2. where it occurs,
3. why it matters scientifically,
4. what kind of evidence, clarification, analysis, or scope change could resolve it.

### Minor comments

Use minor comments for localized clarity, labeling, missing definitions, limited additional analyses, or presentation issues that do not change the central conclusion.

## Be specific

Weak:

> The methods are unclear.

Better:

> The manuscript does not state whether samples from the same participant were modeled as dependent observations; if treated as independent, the uncertainty estimates may be too small. Please clarify the unit of analysis and model structure.

## Prioritize

Do not bury the central validity problem among dozens of copyedits. A concise review with five high-information comments is often more useful than a long list of preferences.

## Balance criticism with actionable guidance

State strengths when scientifically relevant. For weaknesses, offer a path to resolution without micromanaging the authors.

## Focus on science, not style

Mention language or organization when it prevents scientific understanding. Avoid becoming a copyeditor unless the venue explicitly asks for it.

## Keep tone professional

Discuss the manuscript and evidence rather than the competence or motives of the authors.

Prefer:

> The manuscript states X, but the evidence shown supports Y.

over personal or accusatory language.
