# Reading and Evidence Notes

Do not postpone synthesis until after reading. Capture structured evidence and emerging relationships while the context is fresh.

## Minimum extraction for each study

Record:

- research question,
- study design,
- population/system/data,
- intervention or exposure if applicable,
- measurement and outcome definitions,
- analysis method,
- main quantitative or qualitative findings,
- uncertainty,
- limitations,
- claims made by the authors,
- what the evidence actually supports,
- relevance to the review question.

## Keep claim and evidence separate

A paper's abstract conclusion is not the evidence itself. When a study makes a strong claim, identify the observation or estimate that carries the claim and the assumptions needed to connect them.

This is essential when later comparing studies that use different definitions or analytic choices.

## Take relational notes

In addition to paper-specific notes, capture cross-paper thoughts as they arise:

- agrees with study X under condition Y,
- apparent contradiction may be due to measurement difference,
- same mechanism with different terminology,
- method resolves a limitation in earlier work,
- result appears to depend on population or context,
- useful negative evidence.

These relational notes are the raw material of synthesis.

## Protect attribution

Maintain citation identity at note-taking time. Never leave a fact or paraphrase in notes without a source marker and assume it can be reconstructed later.

If preserving a direct quotation for analysis, clearly mark it as a quotation immediately so it cannot later be mistaken for your own prose.

## Read with variable depth

Not every paper needs the same depth. Use quick screening to decide which studies require full extraction.

Deep-read studies that are:

- central to a major claim,
- methodologically influential,
- contradictory to the apparent consensus,
- unusually strong or weak evidence,
- foundational to terminology or theory.
