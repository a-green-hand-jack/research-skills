# Evidence Synthesis

Synthesis is not a sequence of summaries. It is a comparison of studies along dimensions that explain what the literature collectively supports.

## Build comparison dimensions

Choose dimensions that can explain variation in findings, such as:

- population or system,
- experimental condition,
- measurement definition,
- study design,
- model assumptions,
- analysis method,
- follow-up period,
- data quality,
- intervention intensity,
- risk of bias.

A useful synthesis table usually has studies as rows and explanatory dimensions as columns.

## Diagnose disagreement before declaring contradiction

When two papers disagree, ask:

1. Are they answering the same question?
2. Are key variables defined the same way?
3. Are populations or conditions comparable?
4. Is one study measuring a proxy for what the other measures directly?
5. Could design or analysis choices explain the difference?
6. Do confidence intervals or uncertainty actually permit compatible effects?

A disagreement that disappears after conditioning on context is a boundary condition, not merely noise.

## Organize around themes, not papers

Weak synthesis:

> Paper A found X. Paper B found Y. Paper C found Z.

Stronger synthesis:

> Evidence supports X under conditions A and B, while studies using condition C tend to find Y. The difference is consistent with variation in measurement M, although the available designs do not isolate M from confounder N.

Each paragraph should make a claim about the literature and then use multiple sources as evidence.

## Distinguish consensus from prevalence

Many papers repeating the same conclusion do not necessarily provide independent evidence. Check whether studies reuse the same dataset, method, benchmark, cohort, assumption, or citation chain.

Weight evidence by independence and quality, not raw publication count.

## Identify defensible gaps

Useful research gaps include:

- a consequential question that remains unresolved,
- conflicting evidence whose source is unknown,
- a key assumption not directly tested,
- a population or condition where generalization is unsupported,
- a methodological limitation that prevents strong inference,
- a missing comparison needed to distinguish competing explanations.

Weak gap statements include:

- "few studies exist" without explaining why more are needed,
- "no study has combined A and B" without a scientific reason the combination matters,
- "this has not been studied in location X" without reason to expect a different result.
