# Scope and Review Question

A review becomes useful when its scope is narrow enough to support synthesis but broad enough to answer a question readers care about.

## Define the review in one sentence

Use a sentence such as:

> This review evaluates ______ in order to understand ______ for ______.

Specify:

- the scientific phenomenon or intervention,
- the population, system, or domain,
- the comparison or boundary if relevant,
- the intended audience,
- the type of conclusion the review should enable.

## Check whether the topic is reviewable

The topic should be:

- important enough to justify synthesis,
- defined enough that inclusion decisions can be defended,
- supported by enough literature to compare studies,
- sufficiently current that a review adds value rather than merely repeating a recent synthesis.

Before committing, look for existing reviews and identify what they already cover. A new review should usually provide at least one of:

- a new time window,
- a new body of evidence,
- a new conceptual framing,
- a comparison not previously synthesized,
- resolution of a disagreement,
- a bridge between previously separate literatures.

## Choose the review type deliberately

Do not blur narrative and systematic review conventions.

A **narrative/critical review** is appropriate when the main goal is conceptual synthesis, historical development, interpretation, or integration across heterogeneous evidence.

A **systematic review** is appropriate when the question requires a predefined, transparent search and selection protocol to reduce bias.

A **meta-analysis** adds quantitative synthesis when study estimates are sufficiently compatible and the statistical assumptions are defensible.

Do not present a convenience search as systematic.

## Define boundaries before deep reading

Write provisional inclusion and exclusion criteria early. Boundaries can be refined, but unrecorded drift creates a review whose scope changes to fit whatever the author happens to find.

Possible boundaries:

- study design,
- population or organism,
- intervention/exposure,
- outcome,
- method family,
- publication period,
- language,
- evidence type.
