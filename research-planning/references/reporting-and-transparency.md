# Reporting and Transparency

Research planning is incomplete until there is a plan for reporting what actually happened, including deviations, null findings, and uncertainty.

## Report the study that was done

The final report should distinguish:

- original aims,
- prespecified analyses,
- deviations from protocol,
- exploratory analyses added later,
- all primary outcomes,
- relevant negative or inconclusive findings.

Do not silently rewrite the planned study around the most attractive result.

## Avoid selective reporting

Selective publication and selective outcome reporting distort the scientific record. A nonsignificant result is not automatically evidence of no effect, and it should not disappear merely because it is less exciting.

Report results according to their relevance to the research question, not according to whether they cross a threshold.

## Use reporting guidelines when applicable

Study-type reporting guidelines can reveal missing design and analysis details before submission. Use the appropriate guideline or checklist as an engineering aid, not merely a journal-formality step.

## Plan reproducibility artifacts

Before publication, determine what will be shared and how:

- protocol/preregistration,
- data or a justified access path,
- analysis code,
- software environment,
- metadata/data dictionary,
- computational workflow,
- supplementary analyses.

When restrictions apply, report them precisely rather than implying openness that does not exist.

## Match claims to transparency

If key data, methods, or analysis decisions cannot be inspected, the strength of claims may need to reflect that limitation.
