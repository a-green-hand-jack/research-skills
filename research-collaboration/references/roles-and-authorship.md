# Roles, Responsibilities, and Authorship

Ambiguity about who owns which task and how credit will be assigned is a predictable source of conflict.

## Define responsibilities early

For each workstream, state:

- responsible owner,
- contributors,
- expected deliverable,
- dependency on others,
- target date,
- decision rights.

"Everyone is helping" is not a role definition.

## Discuss authorship before the manuscript exists

Early authorship discussions do not require a permanent author order. They should establish expectations about what kinds of contributions merit authorship and what contributions are currently anticipated.

Revisit the discussion when contributions change.

Useful questions:

- Who is expected to lead the paper?
- Who is responsible for the central analysis or experiment?
- Who will draft and revise the manuscript?
- How will major intellectual contributions be recognized?
- How will changes in workload affect author order or contribution statements?

## Credit specific contributions

Do not let invisible labor disappear. Track contributions such as data curation, software, methodology, experimental work, statistical analysis, supervision, visualization, and writing.

When presenting preliminary work, acknowledge who produced the underlying contribution.

## Do not silently expand your task into someone else's domain

New ideas are normal, but changing the plan without discussion can create duplicated effort, ownership disputes, or incompatible analyses.

Before expanding scope, discuss:

- why the new work is needed,
- who should own it,
- whether it changes timelines,
- whether it changes credit.
