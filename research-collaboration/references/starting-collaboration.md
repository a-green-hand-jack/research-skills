# Starting a Collaboration

Do not accept a collaboration merely because the collaborator is prestigious, the grant is attractive, or the project sounds fashionable.

## Check scientific fit

Before committing, ask:

- Does the collaboration answer a question I actually care about?
- Does each party contribute a capability the project needs?
- Is the expected scientific value larger than the coordination cost?
- Do the participants share a compatible standard for rigor and openness?
- Can I realistically deliver the work this collaboration requires?
- Where does this project sit relative to my other commitments?

A polite early "no" is cheaper than a long low-priority collaboration that neither party can finish.

## Define the purpose

Write a short collaboration statement containing:

- scientific objective,
- expected outputs,
- major workstreams,
- dependencies between participants,
- decision-making process,
- expected timeline.

This can be a lightweight memo or shared document. The point is mutual clarity, not legal formality.

## Identify dependency risk

Map tasks that block other tasks. A collaboration is fragile when a single participant owns an essential artifact and has no agreed delivery point or backup.

For critical dependencies, clarify:

- format of the handoff,
- due date,
- quality criteria,
- who can proceed if the original plan fails.

## Leave room for evolution

Scientific projects change. Agree at the beginning that changes to scope, task ownership, timeline, and credit will be discussed explicitly rather than assumed.
